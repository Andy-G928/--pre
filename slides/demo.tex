\section{Research Question}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Motivation: A Dual Role}
  \small
  Many large platforms now play two roles at the same time.

  (Eg. Amazon, JD.com, Apple App Store...)

  \begin{itemize}
    \item They run a marketplace for third-party sellers.
    \item They also sell their own products on the same marketplace.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{The key tension}
    The platform is both the market \textbf{organizer} and one of the \textbf{sellers} in the market.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Why Regulators Worry}
  \small
  The concern is not only that the platform sells its own products.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Product imitation:} the platform may use marketplace data to copy successful sellers.
    \item \textbf{Self-preferring:} the platform may steer consumers toward its own products.
    \item \textbf{Innovation harm:} third-party sellers may invest less if they expect imitation or steering.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Policy question}
    Should regulators ban the platform from selling on its own marketplace?
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Research Question}
  \small
  The paper asks three linked questions.

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Does banning dual mode always raise consumer surplus and welfare?
    \item If dual mode is banned, does the platform switch to seller mode or marketplace mode?
    \item Are targeted rules on imitation and self-preferring better than a full ban?
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{block}{Main message}
    A ban on dual mode can hurt consumers and welfare. Targeting bad conduct is often better.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Roadmap for the Talk}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Motivation and main question
    \item Baseline model setup
    \item Baseline equilibria and price squeeze
    \item Ban on dual mode
    \item Imitation, self-preferring, and policy comparison
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{block}{How to read the model}
    Each mathematical result is followed by its economic intuition.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Baseline Model}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Players}
  \small
  The model studies one product category.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Platform $M$: can run a marketplace, sell its own product, or do both.
    \item Innovative seller $S$: has a better product and can invest in quality.
    \item Fringe sellers: sell a basic product and compete strongly.
    \item Consumers: each buys at most one unit or takes an outside option.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    The platform is important because consumers use it to find products and to trade more easily.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Values and Parameters}
  \small
  Product values are:

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Product & Consumer value & Meaning \\
      \midrule
      Fringe sellers & $\nu$ & Basic product \\
      Innovative seller $S$ & $\nu+\Delta$ & Better product \\
      Platform $M$ & $\nu+\sigma$ & Platform's own product \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item $b>0$: convenience benefit from buying through the platform.
    \item $\nu_o \sim G$: outside option.
    \item $\tau$: platform commission.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    $\sigma>0$ means the platform is a better seller than fringe sellers; $\sigma<0$ means the opposite.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Innovation by Seller $S$}
  \small
  Seller $S$ chooses innovation $\Delta \geq \Delta^l$ and pays fixed cost $K(\Delta)$.

  \begin{equation*}
    K'(\bar{\Delta}) = G(\nu+\sigma+b)
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Meaning of $\bar{\Delta}$}
    $\bar{\Delta}$ is the high innovation level that can arise when the market gives strong rewards to innovation.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    The left side is marginal cost of innovation. The right side is the demand gain from better quality.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Product Discovery}
  \small
  Consumers do not know about $S$ at the start.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Consumers know the fringe sellers.
    \item Consumers may know the platform's own product.
    \item Consumers discover $S$ only if $S$ is available on the platform.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Why this matters}
    The platform is not only a sales channel. It is also a discovery channel.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    If a policy changes the platform's mode, it may also change whether consumers find $S$.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Three Platform Modes}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Mode & What $M$ does & Key feature \\
      \midrule
      Marketplace & Hosts third-party sellers & Earns commission $\tau$ \\
      Seller & Sells its own product & No marketplace for $S$ \\
      Dual & Hosts sellers and sells own product & Both roles at once \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    The policy debate is about whether the third mode should be allowed.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Timing}
  \small
  The game is solved by backward induction.

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $M$ chooses its mode and sets commission $\tau$ if there is a marketplace.
    \item $S$ decides whether to join the platform and chooses $\Delta$.
    \item Sellers set prices.
    \item Consumers choose what to buy.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{block}{Equilibrium concept}
    Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Equilibrium Analysis}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Marketplace Mode}
  \small
  In marketplace mode, $M$ only charges commission $\tau$.

  \begin{equation*}
    p_i \leq \tau+\Delta, \qquad p_o \leq \tau+\Delta-b
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Showrooming constraint}
    If $\tau>b$, seller $S$ can use the platform for discovery and then sell directly.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    The platform can charge at most the extra convenience value $b$ that it gives consumers.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Marketplace Equilibrium}
  \small
  Proposition 1:

  \begin{equation*}
    \tau^{mkt}=b, \qquad
    G(\nu)=K'(\Delta^{mkt}), \qquad
    p_i^*=b+\Delta^{mkt}
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{equation*}
    \Pi^{mkt}=bG(\nu), \qquad
    \pi^{mkt}=\Delta^{mkt}G(\nu)-K(\Delta^{mkt})
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    $M$ sets the highest fee that does not push trade outside the platform. $S$ invests until marginal cost equals marginal market gain.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Seller Mode}
  \small
  In seller mode, the platform only sells its own product.

  \begin{equation*}
    \max_{p_m\leq b+\sigma} p_m G(\nu+b+\sigma-p_m)
  \end{equation*}

  The competitive constraint binds:

  \begin{equation*}
    p_m^*=b+\sigma
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Proposition 2}
    $M$ sells to all consumers, while $S$ sells to no one.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    The platform captures its convenience value and its product advantage, but consumers do not discover $S$.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Dual Mode: Two Pricing Cases}
  \small
  In dual mode, $M$ both charges commission and sells its own product.

  \begin{block}{Case 1: $\sigma \geq \Delta$}
    The platform's own product is good enough. Consumers buy from $M$.
    \[
      p_i^*=\tau, \qquad p_m^*=\tau+\sigma-\Delta
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Case 2: $\Delta>\sigma$}
    Seller $S$ has the better product. But $M$ still puts pressure on $S$'s price.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    Dual mode matters most when $S$ is better, but $M$ can still discipline $S$'s price.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Price Squeeze}
  \small
  In the price squeeze equilibrium:

  \begin{equation*}
    p_i^*=p_m^*+\Delta-\sigma
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{equation*}
    p_m^*\in
    \left[
    \max\{\tau-\Delta+\sigma,0\},
    \tau+\min\{\sigma,0\}
    \right]
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{equation*}
    \Pi=\tau G(\nu+\sigma+b-p_m^*)
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    $M$ may not sell, but its own product caps $S$'s price. A lower $S$ price raises demand and commission income.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Innovation Constraint}
  \small
  Define $\bar{\tau}$ by:

  \begin{equation*}
    (\bar{\Delta}-\sigma-\bar{\tau})G(\nu+b+\sigma)-K(\bar{\Delta})=0
  \end{equation*}

  Lemma 1:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $\tau\leq\bar{\tau}$, then $S$ chooses high innovation: $\Delta=\bar{\Delta}$.
    \item If $\tau>\bar{\tau}$, then $S$ chooses low innovation: $\Delta=\Delta^l$.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    A high commission lowers $S$'s margin. If the margin is too low, innovation is not worth the cost.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Dual Mode Equilibrium}
  \small
  Proposition 3: $M$ balances two limits.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Showrooming constraint: $\tau\leq b$.
    \item Innovation constraint: $\tau\leq\bar{\tau}$.
  \end{itemize}

  If high innovation is profitable enough:

  \begin{equation*}
    \tau^{dual}=\min\{b,\bar{\tau}\}, \qquad \Delta^{dual}=\bar{\Delta}
  \end{equation*}

  Otherwise:

  \begin{equation*}
    \tau^{dual}=b, \qquad \Delta^{dual}=\Delta^l
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    The platform may charge less to keep $S$ willing to innovate, because innovation expands the market.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Which Mode Does the Platform Choose?}
  \small
  Corollary 1:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item $M$ prefers marketplace mode over seller mode iff $\sigma\leq 0$.
    \item Dual mode is always better for $M$ than marketplace mode.
    \item There is a threshold $\underline{\sigma}>0$ such that:
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{equation*}
    \Pi^{dual}>\Pi^{sell}
    \quad \text{iff} \quad
    \sigma<\underline{\sigma}
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    Dual mode creates more transactions through price squeeze. But if $M$'s own product is much better, $M$ may prefer to sell alone.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Ban on Dual Mode}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What Does a Ban Change?}
  \small
  If dual mode is banned, $M$ must choose one pure mode.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $\sigma>0$, seller mode is more attractive.
    \item If $\sigma\leq 0$, marketplace mode is more attractive.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Important point}
    The platform reacts to the policy. It does not keep the same behavior.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Ban in the Baseline Model}
  \small
  Proposition 4:

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      Region & New mode & Consumer surplus & Welfare \\
      \midrule
      $\sigma\geq\underline{\sigma}$ & Seller & no change & no change \\
      $0<\sigma<\underline{\sigma}$ & Seller & lower & lower \\
      $\sigma\leq0$ & Marketplace & lower & unclear \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    When the ban matters, consumers lose the price squeeze effect. If $M$ switches to seller mode, consumers may also lose discovery of $S$.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Why Welfare Can Fall}
  \small
  The key case is $0<\sigma<\underline{\sigma}$.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Before the ban, $M$ uses dual mode.
    \item After the ban, $M$ switches to seller mode.
    \item Consumers no longer discover $S$ through the platform.
    \item Price competition from $S$ is weaker.
    \item Consumers cannot combine $S$'s better product with platform convenience $b$.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    A rule that looks like it protects sellers can hurt consumers, because it may close the discovery channel.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Policy and Extensions}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Adding Two Bad Conducts}
  \small
  The paper then adds the two main policy concerns.

  \begin{block}{Product imitation}
    If $S$ joins the platform, $M$ can copy $S$'s product before prices are set.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Self-preferring}
    After prices are set, $M$ chooses whether to show $S$ to consumers.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    These assumptions give the strongest case against dual mode: copying is perfect and steering is perfect.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Dual Mode with Imitation and Steering}
  \small
  Proposition 5:

  \begin{equation*}
    \Delta^{dual}=\Delta^l
  \end{equation*}

  If $\sigma\geq\Delta^l$:

  \begin{equation*}
    \Pi^{dual}=(b+\sigma)G(\nu)
  \end{equation*}

  If $\sigma<\Delta^l$:

  \begin{equation*}
    \Pi^{dual}=(b+\Delta^l)G(\nu)
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    $S$ does not invest because high innovation can be copied or hidden. $M$ can extract the value through high fees or imitation.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Does a Dual Mode Ban Fix This?}
  \small
  Proposition 6:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $\sigma\geq\Delta^l$: no effect.
    \item If $0<\sigma<\Delta^l$: $M$ switches to seller mode; welfare falls.
    \item If $\sigma\leq0$: $M$ switches to marketplace mode; innovation and welfare rise.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    A structural ban does not directly restore innovation or fair display. It only forces $M$ to pick one pure mode.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Policy 1: Ban Self-Preferencing}
  \small
  If self-preferring is banned, $M$ must show $S$ when $S$ is listed.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item On-platform competition returns.
    \item The showrooming constraint returns.
    \item Final prices fall.
    \item But imitation is still possible, so innovation may stay low.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    This policy fixes the display problem, but not the copying problem.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Policy 2: Ban Imitation}
  \small
  If imitation is banned, $M$ can commit not to copy $S$.

  \begin{equation*}
    \tau\leq\bar{\tau}
  \end{equation*}

  If innovation is valuable enough:

  \begin{equation*}
    \tau=\bar{\tau}, \qquad \Delta=\bar{\Delta}
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    Commitment not to copy can make $S$ invest more. A larger market can also help the platform.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Policy 3: Ban Both}
  \small
  If both imitation and self-preferring are banned, the model is close to the baseline dual mode.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item $S$ is shown to consumers.
    \item $M$ cannot copy $S$.
    \item Price competition returns.
    \item Innovation incentives return.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Intuition}
    The two rules work together: one restores competition, the other restores innovation.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Policy Comparison}
  \small
  Main policy lesson:

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Policy & What it targets & Main effect \\
      \midrule
      Ban dual mode & Market structure & Can remove useful dual mode benefits \\
      Ban self-preferring & Display choice & Restores price competition \\
      Ban imitation & Copying choice & Restores innovation incentives \\
      Ban both & Both conducts & Best at keeping benefits and limiting harm \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    The problem is not always dual mode itself. The problem is how the platform uses its power.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Conclusion}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Main Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Dual mode can create a price squeeze that lowers prices and raises demand.
    \item Banning dual mode can lower consumer surplus and welfare.
    \item Imitation hurts innovation incentives.
    \item Self-preferring weakens platform competition.
    \item Targeted conduct rules are often better than a full structural ban.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{alertblock}{Final message}
    Regulate the harmful conduct, not only the platform's dual role.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}
